# CAT-Q Interpretation — Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire

> Source: Hull, L., Mandy, W., Lai, M.-C., Baron-Cohen, S., Allison, C., Smith, P., & Petrides, K. V.
> (2019). Development and Validation of the Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (CAT-Q). *Journal
> of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, 49(3), 819-833. Scored by `score_cat_q` (noesis-mcp): 25 items,
> 1-7 scale, three subscales (Compensation, Masking, Assimilation), reported as **means** (1-7), not
> percentiles. Use alongside `strength-frames.md`'s framing conventions and `2e-archetypes.md` for
> cross-instrument pattern language.

## What CAT-Q actually measures — and what it doesn't

Camouflaging is the set of strategies people use to appear more socially "typical" than their internal
experience — copying others' body language, rehearsing scripts, monitoring your own face in real time,
forcing yourself through interactions that don't come naturally. CAT-Q was built to measure this directly
in autistic and non-autistic adults alike. Frame it exactly that way: **camouflaging is a strategy people
learn, not a trait people have** — closer to a skill set (with real costs) than a personality dimension.

Two things to say plainly, every time, before anything else:

1. **CAT-Q does not diagnose autism.** The authors themselves state that its clinical utility "requires
   further research to establish," and they publish no diagnostic cutoff. Plenty of non-autistic people
   camouflage too — in job interviews, across cultures, when masking any stigmatized identity, or simply
   under high social stakes. A high score describes *how much conscious effort a person's social presentation
   takes*, not a category they belong to.
2. **A low score does not rule anything out either.** Some people never learned to camouflage. Some
   used to and have consciously stopped (often at real social cost, after deciding the toll wasn't worth
   it). Some are camouflaging so automatically at this point that they've lost conscious awareness of
   doing it — the CAT-Q depends on the respondent noticing their own strategy, which is itself uneven
   across people.

There is no published population norm and no clinical cutoff anywhere in this instrument's own validation
research. Every score below is described relative to the 1-7 scale itself (toward Agree/7, toward
Disagree/1, or in the middle) — not as a percentile, because none exists.

## The three subscales

### Compensation — active strategies to offset social difficulty

Items ask about deliberately copying others' body language/expressions, building and using scripts for
conversations, learning social rules by studying psychology or watching how others interact, and
practicing facial expressions/body language so they "look natural."

**Toward the high end:** You've built a genuine skill set for reading and reproducing social convention —
possibly a substantial one, assembled through deliberate study rather than absorbed automatically. This is
real expertise, not fakery: many autistic (and plenty of non-autistic) adults have effectively taught
themselves social fluency the way someone might teach themselves a language as an adult, through
observation, pattern-matching, and rehearsal. The cost is that it takes conscious bandwidth that comes
"for free" to people who don't need to compensate — bandwidth that isn't available for anything else in
that moment.

**Toward the low end:** You're not spending effort building scripts or studying interaction rules — either
because social interaction already comes easily, or because you've decided compensating isn't worth it for
you, or because building these particular workarounds hasn't been your way of coping with social
difficulty (some people cope by avoiding interaction instead — see Assimilation below, which captures that
more directly).

### Masking — real-time self-monitoring and impression management

Items ask about monitoring your own facial expression/body language "so that I appear relaxed" or
"interested," always thinking about the impression you're making, and (reverse-scored) not needing eye
contact or not tracking what your face/body are doing.

**Toward the high end:** You run a continuous internal monitor on your own presentation during
interaction — checking how you're coming across and adjusting live. This can produce real social
smoothness and is often mistaken by others for effortless ease, when it's actually active, moment-to-moment
work. The strain is cumulative: a channel of attention is permanently allocated to self-observation instead
of being free for the conversation itself, which is a documented contributor to the exhaustion many
autistic adults describe after socializing ("social hangover").

**Toward the low end:** You're not routing attention into monitoring your own face and body during
interaction — you're either genuinely unselfconscious, or your attention during social contact goes
somewhere else entirely (task content, the other person's words, your own internal experience) rather than
to self-presentation. This frees up real cognitive resources for the actual substance of the interaction.

### Assimilation — the felt experience of trying to "pass" or fit in

Items ask about feeling like you're "performing" rather than being yourself, needing others' support to
socialize, forcing yourself to interact, avoiding interaction when possible, and (reverse-scored) feeling
free to be yourself, feeling conversation flow naturally, or not feeling like you're "pretending to be
normal."

**Toward the high end:** Social situations carry a felt sense of pretense or forcing — of putting on
something rather than simply being present. This subscale is less about *technique* (Compensation) or
*monitoring* (Masking) and more about the subjective, felt gap between your inner experience and what
you're presenting outward. That gap is real and worth taking seriously; naming it precisely (rather than
just "I'm bad at socializing") is often the first useful step toward deciding, deliberately, when
camouflaging is worth the cost and when it isn't.

**Toward the low end:** Interaction feels like it flows without a felt sense of performance — you're not
experiencing a gap between your internal state and your outward presentation, whatever the actual content
of that presentation looks like from the outside.

## Reading the three subscales together

Because these are three separate means, not one combined score, the *pattern* across them often carries
more information than any single number:

- **High Compensation + high Masking, lower Assimilation**: real technique and real-time self-monitoring
  are both active, but the person doesn't feel like they're "faking it" while doing so — camouflaging has
  become integrated rather than experienced as pretense. Worth naming as a genuine adaptation, while still
  flagging that the cognitive cost (see below) doesn't disappear just because it doesn't *feel* like
  performing.
- **High Assimilation with lower Compensation/Masking**: the felt sense of forcing/performing is strong
  even without a large deliberate technique repertoire — this can describe someone earlier in
  understanding their own social patterns, or someone whose camouflaging effort goes into avoidance
  (opting out of interaction) rather than active technique.
- **All three low**: consistent with genuinely low camouflaging effort — either social interaction isn't
  effortful for this person in the ways CAT-Q asks about, or camouflaging isn't how they've historically
  coped with any difficulty they do have.
- **All three high**: substantial, active work going into social presentation across every angle the
  instrument asks about — technique, monitoring, and felt performance all elevated together. This is the
  profile most worth pairing with an honest conversation about camouflaging fatigue (below), not just a
  number.

## The cost that doesn't show up in any single number

The camouflaging research literature (starting with this instrument's own authors) consistently connects
sustained high camouflaging to exhaustion, delayed self-understanding or diagnosis, and burnout — precisely
*because* it looks successful from the outside, so the effort stays invisible to everyone but the person
doing it. If someone's scores are elevated across these subscales, the useful question isn't "is this
good or bad" — it's "is this effort sustainable for you, and do the people around you actually know it's
happening?" That's a conversation, not a verdict the score can deliver on its own.

## What this file does not tell you

- **Not a diagnosis, not a screening cutoff.** No band or threshold exists in the source research; this
  file does not invent one.
- **No population or neurodivergence-specific norm.** There is nothing to compare a score against beyond
  the 1-7 scale itself.
- **Doesn't distinguish *why* someone camouflages.** Autism, ADHD, social anxiety, cultural
  code-switching, and masking other stigmatized identities can all elevate these subscales; CAT-Q doesn't
  and can't tell you which is operating for a given person.
- **Self-report depends on self-awareness.** Someone whose camouflaging is deeply automatic may
  under-report it simply because they've stopped noticing they're doing it.
